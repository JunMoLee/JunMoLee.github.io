Semiconductor Device Researcher · Georgia Tech

Junmo Lee

Semiconductor device researcher driving process-to-system co-optimization for next-generation memory and AI hardware development. 

I am a semiconductor device researcher guided by the philosophy that process and device technologies have been and will continue to be the fundamental drivers of transformative advances in AI hardware. This has led me to explore novel oxide-semiconductor and ferroelectric devices for next-generation AI hardware and memory systems through a hands-on, end-to-end approach spanning fabrication, device characterization, system-level design and evaluation. In particular, I am interested in redefining "capacitors" from passive components into active building blocks for storage and computation, leveraging their low leakage, low static power, and charge-based operation as an alternative to the transistor- and resistance-based approaches that underpin most commercial memory and computing technologies today. Feel free to explore my website to learn more about me and my research.

Currently a Ph.D Student at Georgia Tech & Device Engineer / Data Scientist Intern at Intel Corporation (Logic Technology Development)
01 / About

About

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Electrical and Computer Engineering (minor in Physics) at the Georgia Institute of Technology (Georgia Tech), advised by Prof. Shimeng Yu and supported as an SRC Research Scholar. Before joining Georgia Tech, I completed my B.S in Electrical and Computer Engineering at Seoul National University, advised by Prof. Jong-Ho Lee and Prof. Sangbum Kim during my undergraduate research internships. My research has focused on bridging semiconductor process (foundry technology) development and system-level hardware design: I fabricate emerging oxide-semiconductor (ALD W-doped In2O3) and ferroelectric (HfxZr1-xO2) devices, monolithically integrate them onto foundry CMOS, and systematically evaluate their impact at the circuit and system level.

In memory and AI-hardware research, semiconductor process, device technology and circuit/architecture design are often treated as separate layers. My process-to-system approach connects them: I build physical devices through hands-on fabrication, then model and evaluate their impact when integrated into compute-in-memory and 3D-stacked systems. This perspective connects my work from cleanroom fabrication to chip-level benchmarking and guides my long-term goal of bridging foundry technology development with system-level hardware design for future AI hardware.

01 — Process → Device

Oxide-Channel nvCAP Memory Superlattice Capacitors On-chip Power Delivery

Oxide-Semiconductor & Ferroelectric Memory

Non-volatile capacitive memory (nvCAP) is a new memory-device concept that uses capacitance states, rather than conventional resistance or charge states, to encode information. I pioneered an oxide-channel nvCAP incorporating an oxide-semiconductor channel, enabling two key features for future AI hardware: (i) BEOL integration compatibility and (ii) a high capacitance on/off ratio (>20). I developed the ALD processes for the two material systems that enable these characteristics: (i) W-doped In₂O₃ oxide-semiconductor channels and (ii) HZO ferroelectric thin films. Later, I further improved the oxide-channel nvCAP characteristics by dual-gate engineering, followed by comprehensive device characterization, circuit-level evaluation, and an array-level demonstration of charge-domain in-memory search.

On-Chip Vertical Power Delivery: 

BEOL Oxide TFTs and Superlattice Capacitors

Beyond memory devices, I have also contributed to developing BEOL-compatible power delivery systems for the efficient operation of emerging 3D-integrated chip architectures in the AI era. I worked on fabricating on-chip switched-capacitor DC-DC converters built from ALD oxide thin-film power transistors and superlattice MIM capacitors. I developed and optimized the backside-compatible high-voltage superlattice capacitor at the core of this converter, along with comprehensive reliability analysis and time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB) characterization. This device-level work was subsequently integrated into a full on-chip DC-DC converter demonstration developed in collaboration with Dr. S. Deng and Dr. J. Kwak.

02 — Process Integration

Monolithic 3D Integration on Foundry CMOS

A device is truly BEOL-compatible only if it can be integrated directly on top of FEOL Si CMOS. I designed and experimentally demonstrated a monolithic 3D (M3D) integration flow, namely CMOS+X flow, for stacking dual-gated oxide-channel non-volatile capacitive memory (DG nvCAP) directly on TSMC 40 nm Si CMOS, marking the first experimental demonstration of a ferroelectric nvCAP integrated on a foundry CMOS platform. This work was nominated for the Roger A. Haken Best Student Paper Award at IEDM 2025 and was invited for journal publication in IEEE Transactions on Electron Devices.

Dual-gated M3D memory monolithically stacked on 40 nm foundry CMOS

03 — Circuit / System Design

Next-Generation AI Hardware Design

(Digital Compute-in-Memory)

Fabricated memory devices or process integration schemes become useful for AI hardware only once they are built into a functional circuit or system. I have focused on leveraging the fabricated oxide/ferroelectric devices or projected advanced logic transistors to enable new digital compute-in-memory (DCIM) architectures. Capacitive digital compute-in-memory (Cap-DCIM) is an AI accelerator architecture enabled by the monolithically integrated non-volatile capacitive memories on TSMC's 40 nm chip. I also designed a CFET-based 8T DCIM architecture using CFET enabling for A5(0.5 nm)-node 3D digital compute-in-memory: connecting the devices from the earlier stages to circuit-level AI hardware design.

Architecture
Capacitive digital compute-in-memory (Cap-DCIM); CFET-based 8T DCIM bit-cell
Technology
Built on the TSMC 40 nm M3D capacitive memory; 0.5 nm / A5 CFET node

04 — System Benchmark (DTCO / STCO)

Device-to-System Benchmarking 

(DTCO / STCO)

To evaluate circuit- and system-level performance, and ultimately optimize the underlying process and device technologies, a robust device-to-system benchmarking framework is essential. I led the development of NeuroSim V1.4, a C++/Python framework for benchmarking compute-in-memory hardware across technology nodes down to 1 nm. Extended work supporting the A5 technology node with CFET devices was also published. I have used this framework to perform DTCO/STCO of novel digital compute-in-memory architectures, including CFET- and nvCAP-based designs, at leading-edge nodes, connecting device-level assumptions to circuit- and system-level outcomes. NeuroSim V1.4 is widely used in the compute-in-memory research community.
